# Effect of obstructive sleep apnoea and its treatment with continuous positive airway pressure on the prevalence of cardiovascular events in patients with acute coronary syndrome (ISAACC study): a randomised controlled trial

## Summary
Background Despite the improvement in the prognosis of acute coronary syndrome (ACS), substantial morbidity and mortality remain. We aimed to evaluate the effect of obstructive sleep apnoea (OSA) and its treatment with continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP) on the clinical evolution of patients with ACS.
Methods We designed a multicentre, open-label, parallel-group, randomised controlled trial of patients with ACS at 15 hospitals in Spain. Eligible non-sleepy patients were men and women aged 18 years and older, admitted to hospital for documented symptoms of ACS. All patients underwent respiratory polygraphy during the first 24–72 h after admission. OSA patients were randomly assigned (1:1) to CPAP treatment plus usual care (CPAP group) or usual care alone (UC group) by a computerised system available 24 h a day. A group of patients with ACS but without OSA was also included as a reference group. Because of the nature of the intervention, the trial intervention could not be masked to either investigators or patients. Patients were monitored and followed for a minimum of 1 year. Patients were examined at the time of inclusion; after 1 month, 3 months, 6 months, 12 months, 18 months, 24 months, 30 months, and 36 months; and every 12 months thereafter, if applicable, during the follow-up period. The primary endpoint was the prevalence of a composite of cardiovascular events (cardiovascular death or non-fatal events [Acute myocardial infarction, non-fatal stroke, hospital admission for heart failure, and new hospitalisations for unstable angina or transient ischaemic attack]) in patients followed up for a minimum of 1 year. The primary analysis was done according to the intention-to-treat principle. This study is registered with Clinicaltrials.gov, NCT01335087 and is now closed.
Findings Between April 25, 2011, and Feb 2, 2018, a total of 2834 patients with ACS had respiratory polygraphy, of whom 2551 (90·01%) were recruited. 1264 (49·55%) patients had OSA and were randomly assigned to the CPAP group (n=633) or the UC group (n=631). 1287 (50.45%) patients did not have OSA, of whom 603 (46.85%) were randomly assigned to the reference group. Patients were followed up for a median of 3·35 years (IQR 1·50–5·31). The prevalence of cardiovascular events was similar in the CPAP and UC groups (98 events [16%] vs 108 events [17%]; hazard ratio [HR] 0.89 [95% CI 0.68-1.17]; p=0·40) during follow-up. Mean time of adherence to CPAP treatment was 2.78 h/night (SD 2·73). The prevalence of cardiovascular events was similar between patients in the reference group (90 [15%] events) and those in the UC group (102(17%) events) during follow-up (1.01 [0.76-1.35]; p=0.93). The prevalence of cardiovascular events seem not to be related to CPAP compliance or OSA severity. 464 (74%) of 629 patients in the CPAP group had 1538 serious adverse events and 406 (65%) of 626 patients in the UC group had 1764 serious adverse events.
Interpretation Among non-sleepy patients with ACS, the presence of OSA was not associated with an increased prevalence of cardiovascular events and treatment with CPAP did not significantly reduce this prevalence.

## Introduction
Acute coronary syndrome (ACS) is a major health burden, with more than 1 million patients suffering from ACS annually in the USA.1 The short-term and long-term prognoses following diagnosis with ACS have improved (with acute revascularisation and treatment of known cardiovascular risk factors). However, substantial morbidity and mortality remain, including repeat cardiovascular events.
Obstructive sleep apnoea (OSA) is a common disease that affects 20–30% of the adult population2 and is caused by the collapse of the upper airway during sleep. OSA has been associated with an increase in oxidative stress, inflammation, hypercoagulability, and sympathetic activation that could induce cardiovascular disease.3–4 Epidemiological studies have shown that a high proportion (40–60%) of patients with coronary artery disease have OSA.5–7 Previous randomised controlled trials have shown that the treatment of OSA with continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP) is associated with a reduction in blood pressure,8 left ventricular mechanical overload, and arrhythmia.9–11 These effects could improve the prognosis of patients with coronary artery disease.12 However, other studies have shown that the use of CPAP was not associated with reduced risk of cardiovascular outcomes or death for patients with sleep apnoea.13 Evidence suggests that in patients with chronic cardiovascular disease,14 CPAP treatment is not effective as secondary cardiovascular prevention. To gain insight in this area of research,15–18 we designed a secondary prevention trial to evaluate the effect of OSA and the effectiveness of its treatment with CPAP on the clinical evolution and prognosis of patients with ACS.19

## Methods
### Study design and patients
The ISAACC study (Impact of Sleep Apnea syndrome in the evolution of Acute Coronary syndrome. Effect of intervention with CPAP) is a multicentre, open-label, parallel-group, randomised controlled trial of patients with ACS at 15 hospitals across Spain (appendix p 5) The detailed design of this study has been published previously.19
Eligible patients were aged ≥18 years, had been admitted to hospital for ACS with a hospital stay of 24–72 h at the time of sleep study, and had an Epworth Sleepiness Scale (EES) score of ≤10 (ie, patients without excessive daytime sleepiness or non-sleepy). The exclusion criteria included previous treatment with CPAP for OSA; psychophysical inability to complete questionnaires; presence of any previously diagnosed sleep disorder; more than 50% central apnoeas or the presence of Cheyne–Stokes respiration; daytime sleepiness (ESS >10); chronic diseases; a medical history that could interfere with the study objectives or compromise conclusions; any medical factor, social or geographical, that might jeopardise patient compliance; any processes that reduce life expectancy to less than 1 year; and cardiogenic shock. Full eligibility criteria and definitions and the detailed reasons for exclusion during screening are provided in the appendix (p 5).
The Hospital Universitario Arnau de Vilanova in Lleida was the coordinator centre and was responsible for the overall management of the trial. The protocol was approved by the relevant regulatory authorities and ethics committees at each participating site, and patients provided written informed consent.

### Randomisation and masking
Patients with OSA were randomly assigned (1:1) to receive either CPAP therapy plus usual care (CPAP group) or usual care alone (UC group). Simple randomisation was done by a computerised system available 24 h a day via a fully secure, password-protected study website. It was not possible to modify the result in any case. Each time we registered a patient in the database, we randomly assigned them by clicking on the button of the data collection web form. This click produced a call to a command or function “rand ()” within the source code with which the application was implemented, returning a random value. This function was part of the mathematics function library of the standard programming language PHP version 7.3. All used the random number generator of the processor in which the call was executed. That is, rand (minInteger, maxInteger) returned an integer random value between a minInteger and maxInteger, depending on the number of groups of the randomisation. In this case, rand (0,1) returned 0 or 1. As the frequency of patients with AHI less than 15/h is higher than that of patients with AHI at least 15/h and the rate of inclusion into the reference group must match that of the randomised patients, a probabilistic mechanism integrated into the website selected patients once the sleep-test results were known. Because of the nature of the intervention, the trial intervention could not be masked to either investigators or patients.

### Procedures
Eligible patients had respiratory polygraphy (Embletta, ResMed, Bella Vista, NSW, Australia) during the first 24–72 h after admission to hospital to diagnose OSA according to the national guidelines.20 Apnoea was defined as an absence of airflow lasting 10 s or more. Hypopnoea was defined as a reduction in airflow lasting 10 s or more, associated with oxygen desaturation. Oxygen desaturation was defined as a decrease in arterial oxygen saturation of more than 4%. Respiratory polygraphy studies were done without supplemental oxygen. The AHI was defined as the number of episodes of apnoea and hypopnoea per h of recording. The degree of self-reported sleepiness was analysed by the validated Spanish version of the EES.20
Patients diagnosed with OSA (AHI ≥15/h) were then randomly assigned to the CPAP group or UC group. Patients without OSA (AHI <15 / h) were randomly selected to be included in the reference group by means of probabilistic mechanism integrated into the web selected patients once the sleep-test results were known. CPAP pressure was titrated by a trained nurse using automated equipment before discharge, following a validated methodology.21 Patients were instructed in hygienic-dietary measures and in the management of cardiovascular risk factors associated with lifestyle (details are specified in the appendix p 7). All patients were monitored and followed up for a minimum of 1 year. Clinic visits were scheduled for all patients at 1, 3, 6, 12, 18, 24, 30, and 36 months, and annually thereafter.
Sociodemographic and anthropometric variables were recorded, along with those associated with quality of life (EuroQol [EQ-5D]an instrument for measuring quality of life) and degree of daytime sleepiness. At baseline and after a 1-year follow-up, blood samples were obtained during fasting. CPAP adherence was measured by means of the machines’ internal clocks. The degree of compliance was determined by dividing the number of hours of use by the number of days of treatment. Good compliance was defined as CPAP use of at least 4 h a day. Additional details on study measurements and monitoring procedures are provided in the appendix (pp 6–7, 12). Additional details on endpoint adjudication and definitions of primary outcomes are provided in the appendix (p 9-12).

### Outcomes
The primary endpoint of the study was the prevalence of a composite of the first cardiovascular events (cardiovascular death or non-fatal events [acute myocardial infarction, non-fatal stroke, hospital admission for heart failure, and new hospitalisations for unstable angina or transient ischaemic attack]). A committee (see appendix p 11), whose members were unaware of the study group assignments, adjudicated the major cardiovascular outcomes specified in the protocol.
Secondary cardiovascular endpoints included the individual components of the primary composite endpoint, other composites of cardiovascular events (cardiovascular death, myocardial infarction, or stroke), revascularisation procedures, new-onset atrial fibrillation, new-onset diabetes mellitus, and death from any cause. Other secondary outcomes included clinical symptoms of OSA and health-related quality of life. Some of the secondary outcomes listed in the appendix (p 9) are not included in the present analysis, but will be included in future analysis.
Adverse events related to use of CPAP together with serious adverse events were assessed each time the patient was contacted during follow-up. Descriptions of the study endpoints and endpoint adjudication procedures are provided in the appendix (pp 9–12).

### Statistical analysis
Assuming a 25% reduction in the likelihood of further cardiovascular events in the CPAP group compared with that in the UC group, and 10% loss of patients in followup, we estimated that 1264 patients (632 per group) would need to be enrolled to provide 80% power at a two-sided α level of 0·05. Additionally, for comparisons between groups, we determined that a total of 600 patients would be required in the reference group. Thus, the study required 1864 patients.
We estimated descriptive statistics of the mean (SD) with a normal distribution or median with a non-normal distribution (IQR) for quantitative variables, and we used absolute and relative frequencies for qualitative variables. We used Shapiro–Wilks to test normality of continuous variables. We compared both categorical and continuous variables in bivariate analyses with the use of corresponding tests. For the primary analysis, we used an unadjusted Cox proportional regression model according to the intention-to-treat principle based on adjudicated events. In addition, we analysed patients with multiple events by means of Poisson regression. We used one-to-one propensity score matching to match compliant patients (average of ≥4 h/night) in the CPAP group with patients in the UC group who had never used CPAP to estimate the CPAP effect in the adherence set. We analysed continuous variables (ESS, EQ-5D, and blood pressure) by means of mixed linear models to estimate the CPAP effect over time. Moreover, we used a Cox proportional regression model and a Poisson model, adjusted by confounder variables, to estimate the effect of OSA on the primary analysis.
We tested the proportional hazards assumption for all Cox regression models. All tests were two-sided, and we considered p values less than 0·05 as significant. Analysis was done with R statistical software, version 3.5.1 (R Project for Statistical Computing; more details in appendix pp 12–13) This study is registered as ClinicalTrials.gov, NCT01335087.

## Results
Between April 25, 2011, and Feb 2, 2018, a total of 2834 patients with ACS had respiratory polygraphy, of whom 2551 were enrolled. 1264 (49·55%) patients had an AHI of more than 15/h and 1287 (50·45%) had an AHI of less than 15/h (figure 1). Patients with ACS and OSA were randomly assigned to either the CPAP group (n=633) or the UC group (n=631). Nine patients were excluded due to irregularities identified during monitoring, leaving 1255 patients for the primary analysis (n=629 in the CPAP group; n=626 for the UC groups). Of the patients with ACS without OSA, 684 were randomly excluded and 603 were included in the reference group. 596 patients from this group were included in the primary analysis after seven patients were excluded due to irregularities identified during monitoring. The number of OSA patients who discontinued their participation in the study before the intended final visit was 162 (figure 1). The number of patients who completed 1 year of follow-up in each group was 552 (88%) in the CPAP group, 549 (88%) in the UC group, and 511 (86%) in the reference group. The median follow-up period was 3·35 years (IQR 1·50–5·31). The mean age of the patients was 60 years, and 84% were men. The mean body mass index of the patients was 29·5; the mean apneoa–hypopnoea index, 36 events per h; and the mean Epworth Sleepiness Scale score, 5·32 (table 1).
The number of patients who experienced a cardiovascular event during follow-up was not significantly different between the CPAP group and the UC group (98 patients [16%] vs 108 [17%]; hazard ratio [HR] 0·89 [95% CI0.68-1.17]; p=0·40; table 2, figure 2). The annual event rate for cardiovascular events in the CPAP group was 7·18 per 100 person-years compared with 7·78 per 100 person-years in the UC group (relative risk [RR] 0·92 [0.74-1.15]; p=0·47;appendix p 20). The CPAP treatment effect was not influenced by the anthropometric or clinical characteristics of the patients. The mean CPAP adherence was 2·78 h/night (SD 2·73), and the median was 2·2 h/night (IQR 0·03–5·13; appendix pp 17, 37).
A total of 102 (17%) patients in the UC group and 90 (15%) in the reference group had a cardiovascular event during follow-up (HR 1·01 [95% CI 0·76–1·35]; p=0.93). The annual event rate of cardiovascular events was 7·62 per 100 person-years in the UC group and 6·56 per 100 person-years in the reference group (RR 1·16 [0.92-1.47]; p=0·66; appendix p 35). For more on the differences between the usual care group and reference group see the appendix (pp 15, 34–35).
We partitioned patients in the CPAP group into a poor adherence group (mean adherence <4 h/night) or a good adherence group (mean adherence ≥4 h/night) and compared these subgroups with the UC group for prevalence of cardiovascular events. 102 patients had an event (17%) of 607 patients in the UC group, 56 (15%) of 377 in the poor adherence group, and 41 (18%) of 227 in the good adherence group had a cardiovascular event (NB: 25 are missing values; for the poor adherence group vs the UC group, HR 0.88 [0.63-1.22]; p=0·46; for the good adherence group vs the UC group, HR 0·94 [0.65-1.36]; p=0·76). The results of the propensity score analysis showed a HR of 0.80(0.52-1.23; p=0·32 appendix p 22) in the adherent subgroup (for definition of this group see appendix p 12) compared with that in the UC subgroup. Adjustment for the random hospital (site) effect did not modify these results (appendix p 23). Further details on the results for the primary endpoint in the appendix (pp 36, 38–39, 18–23).
The CPAP had no significant effect on the individual components of the primary cardiovascular composite endpoint, composite of major cardiovascular events, revascularisation procedures, all-cause death, atrial fibrillation or other arrhythmias, or newly diagnosed diabetes (table 2).
CPAP treatment was associated with a marginal improvement in ESS and blood pressure and had no effect on quality of life (figure 3). Patients in the CPAP group showed a marginally greater increase in body-mass index and waist circumference throughout followup compared with patients in the UC group. For more secondary and other endpoints (neck circumference, hip circumference, and serious adverse events) see the appendix (p 40).
464 (74%) of 629 patients in the CPAP group had 1538 serious adverse events and 406 (65%) of 626 patient in the UC group had 1764 serious adverse events. The most common serious adverse events were gastrointestinal disorders (113 [18%] in the CPAP group vs 69 [11%] in the UC group), infections and infestations (102 [16%] vs 88 [14%]), and musculoskeletal and connective tissue disorders (97 [15%] vs 104 [17%]; appendix pp 24–33).

## Discussion
The ISAACC study showed that among patients with ACS and OSA, CPAP treatment did not result in a significantly lower prevalence of cardiovascular events than did UC after a median follow-up of 3·35 years. CPAP adherence did not influence these results. Additionally, the presence of OSA was not associated with a worse prognosis in patients with ACS. In contrast, treatment with CPAP was associated with marginal improvement in ESS and blood pressure.
These results should contribute to understanding the observed neutral effect of CPAP treatment for secondary cardiovascular prevention. Three previous randomised controlled trials have directly addressed cardiovascular clinical endpoints in patients who have had a major cardiovascular event. In one single-centre study, the authors assessed the CPAP effect in 244 patients with established coronary artery disease who had just had revascularisation.22 In a second multicentre study that included a total of 140 patients with ischaemic stroke, the effect of CPAP treatment on event-free survival was evaluated.23 None of these studies showed a beneficial effect of CPAP treatment on composite cardiovascular endpoints over several years of follow-up. Finally, the SAVE study14 is a multicentre study that recruited 2717 patients with chronic coronary artery disease or cerebrovascular disease with OSA and evaluated the effect of CPAP treatment. The primary outcome was a similar composite cardiovascular endpoint as in the ISAACC study. The results of the SAVE study showed that after a mean follow-up of 3·7 years, CPAP treatment did not reduce the prevalence of cardiovascular events. Moreover, the absence of an effect on cardiovascular outcomes was reported in multiple prespecified subgroup analyses. As previously reported, both the SAVE and ISAACC studies showed a positive effect of CPAP treatment on clinical symptoms.
A remarkable difference between the studies is that whereas the SAVE study included patients with chronic coronary artery disease or cerebrovascular disease, the ISAACC study recruited patients in the acute phase of coronary disease. Another difference is that the ISAACC study focused on a homogeneous population located in a country with high standards of health care. Notably, the SAVE study did not include a reference group of patients without OSA. The SAVE and ISAACC studies contribute to understanding the observed neutral effect of CPAP treatment for secondary cardiovascular prevention. In these two studies, CPAP treatment showed a neutral effect on patient prognosis, which was expected owing to the absence of deleterious effects of OSA on the prognosis of patients with ACS. We speculate that the potential effect of CPAP treatment would be limited only to primary cardiovascular prevention, with a greater effect observed in patients with adequate adherence than in those without adequate adherence.14,22–24 In patients with OSA and ACS, the identification of independent predictors of recurrent cardiovascular events could contribute in the risk stratification for each patient, and this concept should be explored in further studies. As previously reported,25 we observed that CPAP treatment was associated with a slight increase in body-mass index. The reasons that underline this observation have not yet been determined.
The strengths of our study include its multicentre design with a large patient population. All participating centres used the same methodology, and the sleep study was done with the same polygraph model. Nevertheless, this study has several potential limitations. First, we excluded patients with severe ACS and very poor prognosis (cardiogenic shock) as these patients have poor expectations for short-term outcomes. Additionally, we excluded patients with daytime sleepiness who exhibited the most severe OSA. From an ethical perspective, the OSA patient with an Epworth Sleep Scale score >10 should continue to be treated with CPAP. Our results apply only to non-sleepy OSA patients. Nevertheless, the number of patients excluded for these causes was relatively low (figure 1). Second, patients in this study were predominantly men; therefore, the results cannot be extrapolated to women. Third, the diagnosis is based on respiratory polygraphy, which could underestimate the severity of OSA. However, full polysomnography monitoring could be a stressful procedure for this highrisk patient group. Moreover, although full polysomnography monitoring is the recommended method for the diagnosis of OSA, data from 2017 suggest that respiratory polygraphy could also be used.26 Fourth, patients admitted for ACS could be given pharmacological treatment for the acute phase, including treatment with opioids, that could potentially influence the results of a sleep study by increasing the AHI. Nevertheless, opioids (eg, morphine or meperidine) used for ACS have a short half-life (2 h for morphine and about 2·5–4 h for meperidine). Morphine is used almost exclusively in the first phase of the encounter to relieve chest pain. We did the sleep studies during the first 24–72 h after admission and therefore most, if not all, of the drug would have already been metabolised. Additionally, by excluding central sleep apnoea, the potential confounding factor of opioids is avoided. Fifth, given the very low compliance to CPAP treatment (2·78 h/night), the beneficial effect of sustained well-conducted CPAP (≥4 h/night) cannot be excluded. The patients in this study did not attend any sleep unit , but were instead recruited from coronary units, without being referred for a sleep-related breathing problem or symptoms. In this population, getting a good adherence to CPAP treatment is very difficult, even for experienced research teams. However, as we have found in other studies in this area of research (the RICCADSA22 and SAVE14 studies), the results in the adherent and nonadherent sets do not differ. Sixth, we screened a lower number of patients than originally planned because the prevalence of OSA in the population with ACS was higher than expected and because of the exclusion of those with daytime sleepiness. And finally, we included only white patients and our results cannot be extrapolated to patients with other ethnic or racial backgrounds.

## In conclusion
In a large group of patients with ACS and OSA, the use of CPAP therapy had no positive effect on the prevention of recurrent major cardiovascular events. Moreover, the presence of OSA was not associated with an increased prevalence of cardiovascular events.

# Image Descriptions
## Figure 1: Trial profile
OSA=obstructive sleep apnoea. AHI=apnoea–hypopnoea index. CPAP=continuous positive airway pressure.
The figure is a flow diagram depicting the patient recruitment and study progression for the ISAACC trial.
- Initial screening: 8006 patients with acute coronary syndrome were assessed for eligibility; 5172 were excluded (4410 did not meet inclusion criteria, 762 did not give consent).
- Sleep testing: 2834 patients had validated sleep tests for OSA via respiratory polygraphy; 283 were excluded for having >50% central apnoeas or Cheyne–Stokes respiration.
- Enrollment: 2551 patients were recruited, split into 1264 with OSA (AHI ≥15/h) and 1287 without OSA (AHI <15/h).
- Randomisation for OSA patients: 1264 OSA patients were randomised to CPAP (n=633) or usual care (UC, n=631); 5 excluded from CPAP group and 4 from UC group due to monitoring irregularities, leaving 629 and 626 for primary analysis respectively.
- Reference group selection: 1287 non-OSA patients; 684 randomly excluded, 603 assigned to reference group; 7 excluded due to monitoring irregularities, 596 for primary analysis.
- Study discontinuation: CPAP group (75 discontinued: 66 withdrew consent, 8 lost to follow-up,1 medical reason); UC group (87 discontinued:68 withdrew consent,18 lost to follow-up,1 medical reason); reference group (76 discontinued:65 withdrew consent,11 lost to follow-up).
- Final follow-up: 539 (CPAP), 554 (UC), 520 (reference) patients reached the final follow-up visit.
