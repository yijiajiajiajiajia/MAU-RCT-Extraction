Intensive insulin therapy prevents the progression of diabetic microvascular complications in Japanese patients with non-insulin-dependent diabetes mellitus: a randomized prospective 6-year study
Yasuo Ohkubo*, Hideki Kishikawa, Eiichi Araki, Takao Miyata, Satoshi Isami, Sadatoshi Motoyoshi, Yujiro Kojima, Naohiko Furuyoshi, Motoaki Shichiri
aDepartment of Metabolic Medicine, Kumamoto University School of Medicine, 1-1-1 Honjo, Kumamoto 860, Japan
bDepartment of Ophthalmology, Kumamoto University School of Medicine, 1-1-1 Honjo, Kumamoto 860, Japan
Received 13 January 1995; revision received 20 April 1995; accepted 24 April 1995

Abstract
To examine whether intensive glycemic control could decrease the frequency or severity of diabetic microvascular complications, we performed a prospective study of Japanese patients with non-insulin-dependent diabetes mellitus (NIDDM) treated with multiple insulin injection treatment. A total of 110 patients with NIDDM was randomly assigned to multiple insulin injection treatment group (MIT group) or to conventional insulin injection treatment group (CIT group). Fifty-five NIDDM patients who showed no retinopathy and urinary albumin excretions < 30 mg/24 h at the baseline were evaluated in the primary-prevention cohort, and the other 55 NIDDM patients who showed simple retinopathy and urinary albumin excretions < 300 mg/24 h were evaluated in the secondary-intervention cohort. The appearance and the progression of retinopathy, nephropathy and neuropathy were evaluated every 6 months over a 6-year period. The worsening of complications in this study was defined as an increase of 2 or more steps in the 19 stages of the modified ETDRS interim scale for retinopathy and an increase of one or more steps in 3 stages (normoalbuminuria, microalbuminuria and albuminuria) for nephropathy. The cumulative percentages of the development and the progression in retinopathy after 6 years were 7.7% for the MIT group and 32.0% for the CIT group in the primary-prevention cohort (P=0.039), and 19.2% for MIT group and 44.0% for CIT group in the secondary-intervention cohort (P=0.049). The cumulative percentages of the development and the progression in nephropathy after 6 years were 7.7% for the MIT group and 28.0% for the CIT group in the primary-prevention cohort (P=0.032), and 11.5% and 32.0%, respectively, for the MIT and CIT groups in the secondary-intervention cohort (P=0.044). In neurological tests after 6 years, MIT group showed significant improvement in the nerve conduction velocities, while the CIT group showed significant deterioration in the median nerve conduction velocities and vibration threshold. Although both postural hypotension and the coefficient of variation of R-R interval tended to improve in the MIT group, they deteriorated in the CIT group. In conclusion, intensive glycemic control by multiple insulin injection therapy can delay the onset and the progression of diabetic retinopathy, nephropathy and neuropathy in Japanese patients with NIDDM. From this study, the glycemic threshold to prevent the onset and the progression of diabetic microangiopathy is indicated as follows; HbA₁c < 6.5%, FBG < 110 mg/dl, and 2-h post-prandial blood glucose concentration < 180 mg/dl.

Keywords: Non-insulin-dependent diabetes mellitus; Microvascular complications; Intensive glycemic control; Multiple insulin injection treatment; Glycemic threshold

1. Introduction
After the discovery and clinical application of insulin, death due to acute diabetic complications such as coma has dramatically decreased. However, diabetic microvascular complications such as retinopathy, nephropathy and neuropathy have now been major problems in diabetic patients. The results of the Diabetes Control and Complications Trial (DCCT) in the U.S. and Canada [1,2], as well as those of several other European and American studies, such as the Steno studies [3,4], the Kroc study [5], the Oslo studies [6,7], the Oxford study [8] and the Stockholm studies [9,10], have indicated that intensive insulin therapy could retard the onset and the progression of nephropathy, neuropathy and retinopathy in patients with insulin-dependent diabetes mellitus (IDDM). According to the results of these studies, the significance of intensive glycemic control in IDDM has now been firmly established. However, the significance of intensive insulin therapy in patients with non-insulin-dependent diabetes mellitus (NIDDM) has not been well evaluated.

In Japan, non-obese, insulin-requiring NIDDM patients have been treated with 1 or 2 daily injections of intermediate-acting insulin. However, this conventional insulin injection therapy may be inappropriate for the prevention or intervention of diabetic complications, since conventional insulin injection therapy could not normalize the post-prandial hyperglycemia [11]. Therefore, feasibility of the intensive insulin therapy on the prevention of the onset and the progression of diabetic microvascular complications must be evaluated in NIDDM patients. To address such questions, we designed the Kumamoto study.

The Kumamoto study was a randomized clinical trial, designed to compare intensive insulin therapy, using the multiple insulin injection therapy with the conventional insulin injection therapy, to evaluate their effects on the development and the progression of the microvascular complications in NIDDM patients in both the primary-prevention cohort and the secondary-intervention cohort. The intensive insulin therapy regimen in this study was designed to achieve blood glucose values which were as close to the normal range as possible with the 3 or more daily insulin injections. Two cohorts were designed in order to answer 2 different, but related, questions: Will intensive insulin therapy prevent the development of diabetic microangiopathy in NIDDM patients with no retinopathy and urinary albumin excretion < 30 mg/24 h (primary-prevention), and will intensive insulin therapy affect the progression of microangiopathy in NIDDM patients with simple retinopathy and urinary albumin excretion < 300 mg/24 h (secondary-intervention)? This report presents the results of 6 years of the Kumamoto study in Japanese patients with NIDDM.

2. Patients and methods
2.1. Study design
NIDDM individuals who had been treated with 1 or 2 daily injections of intermediate-acting insulin in our out-patient clinic were asked to participate in this study. A total of 110 patients was selected on the basis of the following criteria: no retinopathy or simple retinopathy determined by clinical funduscopic evaluation, urinary albumin excretion < 300 mg/24 h and serum creatinine level < 1.5 mg/dl, absence of diabetic somatic or autonomic neuropathy severe enough to require treatment, < 70 years of age, and otherwise healthy (no other findings such as hypertension, hypercholesterolemia, severe diabetic complications or other severe medical conditions). All of the patients were diagnosed as being affected with NIDDM by their characteristics of no history of ketoacidosis, negative islet cell antibody, and daily urinary C-peptide excretion more than 20 μg [12]. Informed consent was obtained from each patient.

One hundred and ten patients were divided into 2 cohorts - the primary-prevention cohort (n=55) and the secondary-intervention cohort (n=55). To be included in the primary-prevention cohort, the patients were required to have no retinopathy and urinary albumin excretion < 30 mg/24 h. To be included in the secondary-intervention cohort, the patients were required to have simple retinopathy and urinary albumin excretion < 300 mg/24 h. In addition, these patients were randomly assigned to either conventional insulin injection therapy group (CIT group, n=55) or multiple insulin injection therapy group (MIT group, n=55). The CIT group was administered 1 or 2 daily injections of intermediate-acting insulin. The MIT group was defined as the group that was administered insulin 3 or more times daily (rapid-acting insulin at each meal and intermediate-acting insulin at bedtime). The dosage was adjusted according to the self-monitored results of blood glucose.

The clinical goal of the CIT group was to show no symptoms of hyperglycemia or hypoglycemia, and the glycemic control as close to the fasting blood glucose concentration of < 140 mg/dl. The goal of the MIT group was to maintain the blood glucose control as close to the fasting blood glucose concentration (< 140 mg/dl), 2-h postprandial blood glucose concentration < 200 mg/dl, hemoglobin A₁c (HbA₁c) < 7.0% and mean amplitude of glycemic excursions (MAGE) < 100 mg/dl.

The baseline characteristics of the study cohorts are shown in Table 1. After 6 years, 102 patients remained in the study, 3 patients died (2 in the MIT group and 1 in the CIT group), 3 patients had moved to another city (1 in the MIT group and 2 in the CIT group), and 2 patients had changed from conventional insulin injection therapy to multiple insulin injection therapy. There was no significant difference at entry in either the primary-prevention cohort or the secondary-intervention cohort between the CIT and MIT groups with regard to sex distribution, age, duration of diabetes, body mass index (BMI), insulin dosage, urinary C-peptide excretion, HbA₁c, blood pressure, serum cholesterol, serum triglyceride, serum HDL cholesterol, degree of retinopathy, urinary albumin excretion, urinary N-acetyl-β-D-glucosaminidase (NAG) excretion and nerve conduction velocity. Follow-up examinations of glycemic control and diabetic microvascular complications were performed at 3, 6, 12, 18, 24, 30, 36, 42, 48, 54, 60, 66 and 72 months after initiation of the study.

2.2. Evaluation of glycemic control
Metabolic control was estimated by HbA₁c, fasting blood glucose (FBG), mean blood glucose (MBG), M-value and MAGE from diurnal blood glucose profiles which consisted of 11 tests (fasting, 1 and 2 h post-prandial, bedtime and fasting at the next morning) in 1-day hospitalization periodically. The mean values for HbA₁c, FBG, MBG, M-value and MAGE during the study were calculated to evaluate the glycemic control during a period of 3-72 months. Severe hypoglycemia is defined as an event with symptoms consistent with hypoglycemia in which the patients required the assistance of another person, and which was associated with a blood glucose level < 50 mg/dl and prompt recovery after intravenous glucose loading.

2.3. Evaluation of diabetic microvascular complications
Retinopathy. All of the patients had direct ophthalmoscopy, with pupils dilated. Fundoscopic findings were evaluated by at least 2 examiners (an ophthalmologist and an internist) followed by color fundus photography and fluorescein angiography. The degrees of retinopathy were determined by the 2 eye examiners for each patient in accordance with the ETDRS interim scale [13-15], in which a scale of 19 stages is used to represent the overall extent of retinopathy in both eyes (Table 2).

The development and the progression of retinopathy were defined as the change of at least 2 steps from stage 1 in the primary-prevention cohort, and as 2 or more steps change from stages 2-5 in the secondary-intervention cohort. This definition was chosen because it represented a measure of clinically important worsening.

Nephropathy. The patients with nephropathy were divided into 3 stages depending on their urinary albumin excretion - normoalbuminuria (< 30 mg/24 h), microalbuminuria (30-300 mg/24 h) or albuminuria (> 300 mg/24 h) [3,16]. The change from any stage to a higher stage was defined as progression. Whenever the urinary albumin excretion exceeded 30 mg/24 h in patients with normoalbuminuria, the urine was analyzed for the presence of bacteria. If bacteria were present, a subsequent 24-h collection of urine was obtained after treatment for urinary tract infections. On the other hand, to evaluate the renal tubular function, the 24-h urinary NAG excretion was measured every 6 months and compared between the values at entry and those after 6 years.

Neuropathy. Peripheral nerve function was evaluated by temperature-controlled nerve conduction velocity in the median nerve on one side and by vibration threshold on the radial styloid process of the arm and the medial malleolus of the leg on both sides. The measurements of vibration threshold were taken 3 times on both sides and their averages were calculated. These neurological indices at entry and after 6 years of the study in both the CIT and the MIT group were compared in the combined cohort.

The coefficient of variation (C.V.) of R-R interval and the postural hypotension were used to evaluate the indices of the function of parasympathetic and sympathetic nerves, respectively. The C.V. of R-R interval on electrocardiogram was evaluated both during 100 pulses of normal breathing after 5 min of bed rest and during 100 pulses while deep breathing. In the postural hypotension, blood pressure was measured while the patient was in the supine position after 5 min of bed rest and after standing. The decrease in systolic blood pressure caused by standing was determined. These neurological indices at entry and after 6 years of the study in both the CIT and MIT groups were compared in the combined cohort.

2.4. Evaluation of the relationship between the worsening of retinopathy and nephropathy, and glycemic control indices
The relationship between the worsening of retinopathy and nephropathy, and glycemic control indices, expressed as HbA₁c, FBG and 2-h post-prandial blood glucose concentration, was evaluated in the combined cohort. The crude rate within the 6 grades evaluated according to their glycemic control indices was calculated and expressed as 100 patient-years. The regression lines estimated as a function of the log of mean glycemic control indices were calculated.

2.5. Assays
Blood glucose concentration was assayed using the GOD-PAP method (Autoanalyzer TM, Miles Inc., Tarrytown, NY, USA). HbA₁c value was assayed using high performance liquid chromatography (HLC 723 GHb type 2, Tosoh Co. Ltd, Tokyo, Japan, normal range: 4.8 to 6.4%). Fundoscopic findings were recorded using the CR-45 camera (Cannon Co. Ltd., Tokyo, Japan). Urinary albumin excretion was measured by turbidimetric immunoassay (Automatic analyzer 7150 type, Hitachi Ltd., Tokyo, Japan). Urinary NAG excretion was measured by a calorimetric method using p-nitrophenyl-acetyl-β-D-glucosaminide substrates (SSR-XT, Miles Inc., Tarrytown, NY, USA). Nerve conduction velocity, vibration threshold and C.V. of R-R interval were measured by the Neuromatic 2000 TM (Dantec, Skovlunde, Denmark), the Vibratory Sensation Meter (TM-31A, Medic International, Tokyo, Japan) and the Electro Cardiograph Analysis System 12 (Nihon Kohden Co. Ltd., Tokyo, Japan), respectively.

2.6. Statistical analysis
The Wilcoxon rank-sum test was used to compare the 2 treatment groups within the cohort with regard to ordinal and numerical variables, and the chi-square test was used for comparisons of categorical variables. The life-table method was used to estimate the cumulative incidence of events, with adjustment for periodically timed assessments. The difference between 2 cumulative incidence curves was tested by the Mantel (log-rank) test [17]. The percentages of risk reduction by intensive glycemic control using multiple insulin injection therapy were calculated by subtracting the relative risk of multiple insulin injection therapy as compared with conventional insulin injection therapy from 1 and multiplying by 100. The relative risks of retinopathy and nephropathy were estimated as the crude relative risk in the primary-prevention cohort, the secondary-intervention cohort and the combined cohort. In addition, the relative risks were also calculated using proportional-hazards analysis only in the combined cohort. Event rates of complications were presented as the number of events per 100 patient-years, based on the ratio of observed number of events to the total number of patient-years of exposure. All outcomes were analyzed on the basis of the original treatment assignments. All results were expressed as mean ± S.D. P < 0.05 was considered to be statistically significant.

3. Results
3.1. Glycemic control
As shown in Fig. 1, near normoglycemia was obtained in the MIT group during the third month after the initiation of intensive insulin therapy and was maintained over the 6 years of the study, while the glycemic control in the CIT group did not change significantly. The resultant mean values of FBG, HbA₁c, MBG, M-value and MAGE over 6 years were significantly (P < 0.001) lower in the MIT group than in the CIT group (FBG, 126 ± 36 vs. 164 ± 50 mg/dl; HbA₁c, 7.1 ± 1.1 vs. 9.4 ± 1.5%; MBG, 157 ± 34 vs. 221 ± 45 mg/dl; M-value, 18 ± 10 vs. 49 ± 19; MAGE, 91 ± 30 vs. 128 ± 36 mg/dl). On the other hand, there was a slight but not significant decrease in insulin dosage from the baseline to 6 years in the MIT and CIT groups (MIT group, 0.40 ± 0.28 to 0.36 ± 0.35 U/kg of body weight/24 h; CIT group, 0.41 ± 0.28 to 0.44 ± 0.35 U/kg of body weight/24 h).

3.2. Retinopathy
Primary-prevention cohort. The cumulative percentage of patients with the development of retinopathy after 6 years in the MIT group was significantly lower when compared with those in the CIT group (7.7 vs. 32.0%, P=0.039, Fig. 2A). During the 6-year period, the development of retinopathy appeared in 2 patients in the MIT group and 8 patients in the CIT group. No patient of either group required photocoagulation therapy.

Secondary-intervention cohort. The cumulative percentage of patients with the progression of retinopathy was 19.2% in the MIT group and 44.0% in the CIT group after 6 years (P=0.049, Fig. 2B). During the 6-year period, the progression of retinopathy was found in 5 patients in the MIT group and 11 patients in the CIT group. Two patients in the MIT group and 2 patients in the CIT group were diagnosed as having preproliferative retinopathy, and 1 patient in the CIT group showed proliferative retinopathy during this study. All of them required photocoagulation therapy.

Combined cohort. In the combined cohort consisting of both the primary-prevention cohort and the secondary-intervention cohort, the cumulative percentage of patients with the worsening in retinopathy after 6 years was significantly lower in the MIT group when compared with those in the CIT group (13.4 vs. 38.0%, P=0.007). Intensive glycemic control by multiple insulin injection therapy reduced the average risk of worsening in retinopathy by 69% (95% confidence interval, 24 to 87%) during the entire study period. All of the patients who had serious retinopathy requiring photocoagulation in both the MIT and the CIT groups showed poor glycemic control (the mean value of HbA₁c was 9.6 ± 1.0%).

3.3. Nephropathy
Primary-prevention cohort. The cumulative percentage of patients with the development of nephropathy after 6 years was significantly lower in the MIT group than in the CIT group (7.7 vs. 28.0%, P=0.032, Fig. 3A). During the 6-year period, 5 patients in the CIT group and 2 patients in the MIT group developed microalbuminuria, and 2 patients in the CIT group developed albuminuria.

Secondary-intervention cohort. The cumulative percentage of patients with the progression of nephropathy was significantly lower in the MIT group than in the CIT group (11.5 vs. 32.0%; P=0.044), as shown in Fig. 3B. During the 6-year period, 6 patients in the CIT group and 3 patients in the MIT group developed microalbuminuria, and 2 patients in the CIT group developed albuminuria.

Combined cohort. In the combined cohort, the cumulative percentage of patients with the worsening in nephropathy after 6 years was significantly lower in the MIT group than in the CIT group (9.6 vs. 30.0%, P=0.005). Intensive glycemic control by multiple insulin injection therapy reduced the average risk of worsening in nephropathy by 70% (95% C.I., 14-89%) during the entire study period. On the other hand, the mean urinary NAG excretion in the CIT group increased from 7.3 ± 2.5 U/24 h at the beginning of the study to 7.7 ± 4.2 U/24 h after 6 years, but decreased in the MIT group from 7.2 ± 2.4 U/24 h at entry to 6.2 ± 2.1 U/24 h after 6 years. After 6 years, the difference between the 2 groups was significant (P < 0.05).

3.4. Neuropathy
Results of nerve conduction velocity, vibration threshold, postural hypotension and C.V. of R-R interval in the combined cohort at entry and after 6-year period are shown in Table 3.

The median nerve conduction velocities (both motor and sensory nerves) significantly increased after 6 years in the MIT group as compared with the values at entry, while the median sensory nerve conduction velocity significantly decreased in the CIT group. There were significant differences in the motor and sensory nerve conduction velocities between both groups after 6 years of the study (P < 0.05). The vibration thresholds in the MIT group showed a slight, but not significant increase after 6 years, while those in the CIT group significantly (P < 0.05) increased after 6 years. There were significant (P < 0.05) differences in arm vibration thresholds between the MIT and the CIT groups after 6 years of the study.

The MIT group showed only slight improvements in the postural hypotension and the C.V. of R-R interval in both at rest and during deep breathing, while the CIT group showed slight deterioration in both tests.

3.5. Evaluation of the relation between the worsening of retinopathy and nephropathy, and glycemic control indices
Comparison of glycemic control indices, with or without worsening of retinopathy and nephropathy, are shown in Table 4. The patients without worsening of microangiopathy over the study period exhibited significantly (P < 0.001) lower mean glycemic control indices than the patients with worsening. We analyzed the relationship between the rate of worsening of retinopathy and nephropathy, and glycemic indices expressed as HbA₁c, FBG and 2-h post-prandial blood glucose concentration in the combined cohort (Fig. 4). These analyses showed a continuously increasing risk of the worsening of retinopathy and nephropathy with increasing HbA₁c, FBG and 2-h post-prandial blood glucose concentration. No worsening of retinopathy and nephropathy was observed in the NIDDM patients whose HbA₁c, FBG and 2-h post-prandial blood glucose concentration were below 6.5%, 110 mg/dl and 180 mg/dl, respectively.

3.6. Hypoglycemia
Over the entire study period, 6 patients in the MIT group and 4 patients in the CIT group had 1 or more episodes of mild hypoglycemic reactions, but no patient experienced episodes of coma, seizure, or severe hypoglycemia that required the assistance of another person.

4. Discussion
The fact that the microvascular complications associated with IDDM can be prevented or delayed by intensive insulin therapy [1-10], has provided us with tremendous hope for the future management of patients with diabetes. One of the important questions raised by these studies evaluated in IDDM is to what extent their results can or should be extrapolated to the management of patients with NIDDM. To address such a question, we studied the effect of intensive glycemic control on diabetic microvascular complications in Japanese patients with NIDDM.

In this study, we demonstrated that intensive glycemic control with multiple insulin injection therapy effectively delayed the onset and the progression of diabetic retinopathy, nephropathy and neuropathy in Japanese NIDDM patients. Table 5 demonstrates the reduction in risk for the development and the progression of diabetic microvascular complications in the Kumamoto study for NIDDM in comparison with that in DCCT for IDDM. Although different statistical methods were applied in the Kumamoto study and DCCT because of the different number of patients studied, the results of reduction in risk with intensive insulin therapy in the Kumamoto study for NIDDM patients were almost comparable to those in DCCT for IDDM patients.

In the evaluation of retinal findings, we used the ETDRS interim scale of 19 stages, different from DCCT in which a scale of 25 stages was used [2]. This classification system allowed us to evaluate the retinal findings systematically and effectively. The cumulative percentage of patients showing the worsening in retinal findings was significantly lower in the MIT group than in the CIT group. With intensive insulin therapy, the risks of retinopathy calculated by crude relative risk were reduced by 76, 56 and 65% in the primary-prevention, the secondary-intervention and the combined cohorts. The risk reduction calculated by proportional-hazard analysis in the combined cohort in the Kumamoto study was similar to that in DCCT (69 vs. 63%, respectively). Intensive insulin therapy reduced the risk of severe non-proliferative or proliferative retinopathy by 40% and that of treatment of photocoagulation by 40%. From these results, we confirmed that the near-normal glycemic control was associated with the delay in the development and the progression of diabetic retinopathy. In previous studies of IDDM patients [2,5,18,19], the appearance of soft exudates or intra-retinal microvascular abnormalities has been reported after the initiation of intensive insulin therapy. Initial worsening has been reported to occur during the first year after the start of the intensive insulin therapy. In our study, however, only 1 patient in the MIT group showed the appearance of small bleeding within 6 months after the initiation of intensive therapy. This small bleeding disappeared over the course of the continued intensive insulin therapy.

Intensive glycemic control was also effective for diabetic nephropathy. Intensive insulin therapy reduced the risk of microalbuminuria by 62% and albuminuria by 100% in the primary-prevention cohort. It also reduced the risk of microalbuminuria by 52% and albuminuria by 100% in the secondary-intervention cohort, respectively. The risk reduction calculated by proportional-hazard risk analysis was more in the Kumamoto study for NIDDM than that in DCCT (risk reduction in the combined cohort, 70 vs. 39-54%, respectively). A striking difference in the risk reduction in nephropathy was observed between the Kumamoto study for NIDDM and DCCT for IDDM. The reason why the intensive glycemic control significantly reduced the risk of progression of microalbuminuria or albuminuria in the Kumamoto study has not been explained thoroughly, but might possibly be explained by the different susceptibility to nephropathy between the types of diabetes, NIDDM and IDDM, and between the races, Caucasian and Asian. It has been known that Japanese NIDDM patients are susceptible to nephropathy [20,21]. Further studies on the multicenter randomized prospective study with many Japanese NIDDM patients should, anyhow, be necessary to solve this question. The decrease in the 24-h urinary NAG excretion in the MIT group also supported a beneficial effect of intensive glycemic control on renal tubular function. In previous studies [22-24], high blood pressure has been reported to be responsible for the impairment of the renal function. In our study, blood pressure in both groups did not significantly change over the study period and there were no significant differences in mean blood pressure between those with and without worsening in nephropathy (data not shown). Longer follow-up studies might be needed to elucidate the effect of blood pressure on the progression of nephropathy.

After the 6-year period, there were significant differences between the CIT and MIT groups in the nerve conduction velocities and arm vibration threshold. The postural hypotension and C.V. of R-R interval in the MIT group tended to improve after 6 years, while these indices in the CIT group tended to deteriorate. Therefore, we concluded that intensive glycemic control would have a beneficial effect on both somatic and autonomic nerve function. Our Kumamoto study suggested that somatic nerve function seemed to improve more easily than autonomic nerve function by intensive insulin therapy. This finding was consistent with the findings that reversible changes were possible only in nerve fibers that were myelinated [25,26].

Since DCCT reported that the relative youth of the patients made the detection of treatment-related differences in rates of macrovascular events unlikely [2], the effect of intensive glycemic control on macroangiopathy in NIDDM patients in the Kumamoto study was also evaluated. There were no significant differences in blood pressure levels and lipid profiles between the MIT and CIT groups. Levels of serum cholesterol, triglycerides and HDL cholesterol in both the MIT and CIT groups did not significantly change during the 6-year period. At the initiation of the study, no patient in either the MIT or CIT groups showed abnormal findings in ECG examination. In the MIT group, during the 6-year follow-up period, 1 patient died suddenly, probably due to myocardial infarction, and another patient complained of intermittent claudication. In the CIT group, 1 patient died of cerebral infarction, another patient developed angina pectoris and 2 patients complained of intermittent claudication. The total number of major cardiovascular, cerebrovascular and peripheral vascular events in the CIT group was twice that of the MIT group (1.3 vs. 0.6 events per 100 patient-years). It has been well known that atherosclerosis occurs earlier in diabetic patients than in non-diabetic patients [27,28]. Fu et al. [29] reported that the relative risk of the development of heart disease significantly increased 1.17-fold for every 1% of increase of HbA₁c. Although this might be open to discussion because of the rather smaller number of patients in the Kumamoto study, intensive glycemic control seems to have a beneficial effect on the progression of macrovascular complications.

It has been reported that intensive insulin treatment can induce side-effects, such as hypoglycemia or weight gain. DCCT showed that the risk of severe hypoglycemia was 3-fold higher with intensive insulin therapy than that with conventional insulin therapy [30]. However, in our study, 6 patients in the MIT group and 4 patients in the CIT group had 1 or more episodes of milder hypoglycemic reactions. This might be due to the difference in insulin dosage between our study for NIDDM and DCCT for IDDM. Dosage of insulin in the MIT group was about 0.4 U/kg of body weight/24 h for NIDDM patients in the Kumamoto Study, while the dosage was 0.7 U/kg of body weight/24 h for IDDM patients in DCCT. During the 6-year follow-up study in our Kumamoto study, all patients were assigned to intensive care - visiting an out-patient diabetes clinic every 2 weeks and adjusting insulin dosage according to their degrees of glycemic control. There was a slight but not significant increase in BMI in both the MIT and CIT groups from the baseline to 6 years (MIT group, 20.5 ± 2.1 to 21.2 ± 2.1 kg/m²; CIT group, 20.3 ± 2.8 to 21.9 ± 2.8 kg/m²). This increase was also smaller than that reported from DCCT [2,31] and the Stockholm study [9].

Clinically, it is important to indicate the glycemic threshold to prevent the development and progression of diabetic microvascular complications. No worsening of retinopathy and nephropathy was observed in NIDDM patients whose HbA₁c, FBG and 2-h post-prandial blood glucose concentration were below 6.5%, 110 mg/dl and 180 mg/dl, respectively. Therefore, the glycemic threshold to prevent the onset and progression of diabetic microangiopathy is indicated as follows; HbA₁c < 6.5%, FBG < 110 mg/dl, and 2-h post-prandial blood glucose concentration < 180 mg/dl.

The present randomized prospective study with a limited number of NIDDM patients was performed in the Department of Metabolic Medicine, Kumamoto University School of Medicine, collaborated with 2 University-affiliated hospitals. A complex multicenter, randomized study supported by Diabetes Mellitus Research Grants, the Ministry of Health and Welfare (Japan), is now in progress to draw the final conclusion on the relationship between diabetes control and complications in NIDDM patients. As stated in the report from DCCT [2], intensive insulin therapy in our study was also successfully carried out by an expert team of diabetologists, nurses and dietitians, and the time, effort and cost required were considerable. Exercise therapists and behavioral specialists did not participate in our study.

In conclusion, intensive glycemic control can delay the onset and the progression of the early stages of diabetic retinopathy, nephropathy and neuropathy in NIDDM patients as well as in IDDM patients. It remains, however, to solve the goal of glycemic control or glycemic threshold in patients with advanced microvascular complications, since it has been known that intensive glycemic control deteriorates the advanced stage of diabetic microvascular complications, such as pre-proliferative or proliferative retinopathy and nephropathy showing an albumin excretion of > 300 mg/24 h [32-37].

# Image Descriptions (txt format)
## Figure 1
This figure shows the glycemic control status assessed by five indices (FBG, HbA₁c, MBG, M-value, and MAGE) over a 72-month (6-year) period in NIDDM patients receiving intensive insulin injection therapy (MIT group, indicated by open circles) and conventional insulin injection therapy (CIT group, indicated by closed circles). The x-axis represents the study duration in months (0, 12, 24, 36, 48, 60, 72). The y-axes vary by index: FBG and MBG are in mg/dl (ranging from 50 to 300), HbA₁c is in % (ranging from 6 to 12), M-value ranges from 0 to 60, and MAGE is in mg/dl (ranging from 0 to 200). Data are presented as mean ± S.D. The MIT group achieved near normoglycemia by the third month and maintained it throughout the 6 years, with significantly lower mean values of all five indices compared to the CIT group (P < 0.05 vs. conventional insulin injection therapy). The CIT group's glycemic control indices showed no significant changes over the study period.

## Figure 2
This figure displays the cumulative incidences of retinopathy worsening in NIDDM patients treated with intensive (solid line) and conventional (dashed line) insulin injection therapy. A change in retinopathy severity is defined as an increase of at least 2 steps from the baseline on the ETDRS interim scale. Panel A shows the primary-prevention cohort: the cumulative percentage of retinopathy development after 6 years is 7.7% in the MIT group and 32.0% in the CIT group (P=0.039). Panel B shows the secondary-intervention cohort: the cumulative percentage of retinopathy progression after 6 years is 19.2% in the MIT group and 44.0% in the CIT group (P=0.049). Dropout patients are indicated by short vertical lines on the solid and dashed lines. P-values were tested using the Mantel (log-rank) test.

## Figure 3
This figure presents the cumulative incidences of nephropathy worsening in NIDDM patients treated with intensive (solid line) and conventional (dashed line) insulin injection therapy. A change in nephropathy severity is defined as an increase of 1 or more stages among the three stages (normoalbuminuria, microalbuminuria, albuminuria). Panel A shows the primary-prevention cohort: the cumulative percentage of nephropathy development after 6 years is 7.7% in the MIT group and 28.0% in the CIT group (P=0.032). Panel B shows the secondary-intervention cohort: the cumulative percentage of nephropathy progression after 6 years is 11.5% in the MIT group and 32.0% in the CIT group (P=0.044). Dropout patients are indicated by short vertical lines on the solid and dashed lines. P-values were tested using the Mantel (log-rank) test.

## Figure 4
This figure illustrates the relationship between the rate of worsening of retinopathy and nephropathy, and glycemic control indices (HbA₁c, FBG, 2-h post-prandial blood glucose concentration) in the combined cohort. There are two main sections: 1) Retinopathy and 2) Nephropathy. Each section includes three subplots corresponding to the three glycemic indices:

- For HbA₁c (x-axis: 5-11%), FBG (x-axis: 80-200 mg/dl), and 2-h post-prandial blood glucose concentration (x-axis: 140-300 mg/dl), the y-axis represents the rate of worsening (events per 100 patient-years).

- Squares in each subplot indicate the crude rate within the 6 grades evaluated according to glycemic control levels, with each square corresponding to more than 100 patient-years.

- Solid lines are regression lines estimated as a function of the log of the mean glycemic control indices.

The analyses show a continuously increasing risk of retinopathy and nephropathy worsening with increasing HbA₁c, FBG, and 2-h post-prandial blood glucose concentration. No worsening was observed in patients with HbA₁c < 6.5%, FBG < 110 mg/dl, and 2-h post-prandial blood glucose concentration < 180 mg/dl.
