University Group Diabetes Program
A Study of the Effects of Hypoglycemic Agents on Vascular Complications in Patients with Adult-onset Diabetes
VI. Supplementary Report on Nonfatal Events in Patients Treated with Tolbutamide

Careful review of data available on patients enrolled in the University Group Diabetes Program (UGDP) led the study investigators to conclude in 1969 that the combination of diet and tolbutamide therapy was no more effective than diet alone in prolonging life and possibly less effective than diet alone or than diet and insulin insofar as cardiovascular mortality was concerned. The bases for these conclusions were presented in previous reports from the UGDP.1-3

Only limited information on nonfatal events for patients treated with tolbutamide and diet compared with patients treated with diet alone and patients treated with diet plus insulin was presented in the earlier reports2,3 from the UGDP. The purpose of this report is to summarize for these patient groups the data on nonfatal events collected at scheduled follow-up examinations through the follow-up examination at which tolbutamide treatment was discontinued. Particular attention has been given to the occurrence of events indicative or suggestive of cardiovascular disease in view of the excess cardiovascular mortality of approximately 1 per cent per year observed for patients treated with tolbutamide compared with patients treated with diet alone or treated with diet plus insulin.

METHODS
Design
The design of this prospective study of patients with adult-onset non-insulin-dependent diabetes has been described in detail in earlier reports,1-3 but a few points will be reiterated here. Results for only the 823 patients randomly allocated to the four treatment groups outlined in table 1 are included in this report. The number of patients in each of the four treatment groups was approximately equal-205 in PLBO (placebo), 204 in TOLB (tolbutamide), 210 in ISTD (insulin standard), and 204 in IVAR (insulin variable). The results for patients allocated to phenformin treatment are not considered in the evaluation of tolbutamide therapy because this treatment was added 18 months after recruitment was started for the other four treatment groups and because it was not used in all clinics. An evaluation of phenformin therapy based on findings for fatal and nonfatal events was presented and discussed in other UGDP reports.4,5

Closing Date of Analysis
To include all data on nonfatal events for patients assigned to tolbutamide or to the corresponding placebo treatment group through the quarterly follow-up examination at which the use of tolbutamide was discontinued, the results for all the follow-up examinations performed in the UGDP clinics on or before October 31, 1969, were utilized for all the analyses presented in this report. Results previously reported1-3 for these patients were based on information processed by the Coordinating Center as of October 7, 1969. Inclusion of the data from all examinations performed before October 31, 1969, as compared with the information processed by the Coordinating Center as of October 7, 1969, adds at least one, and in some cases two, quarterly follow-up examinations for all active patients because some time was required to complete each examination within the clinic and to forward the forms to the Coordinating Center. Additional time was required to obtain the lipid results and the readings of ECGs, fundus photographs, and x rays.

The difference in cut-off dates has a small but insignificant effect on the mortality findings. No deaths occurred among the placebo and tolbutamide-treated patients in the interval October 7 through 31, 1969. However, seven patients, two in the placebo-treated group, three in the insulin standard-treated group, and two in the insulin variable-treated group, died prior to October 7, 1969, but the clinics did not learn of these deaths until 1970. The deaths in each of the insulin groups were attributed to cardiovascular causes. One death in the placebo-treated group was attributed to cardiovascular causes and one to cancer. Autopsy information on one death in the placebo-treated group that was counted as a cardiovascular death in previous reports2,3 led to the determination of the review team that this death should be ascribed to noncardiovascular causes.

Data Collected at Each Examination
During the month before initiation of treatment each patient was instructed in an appropriate diet and then given a general physical examination and detailed examinations of the eyes, heart, kidney function, and peripheral vascular system. These data provided the baseline information. At the end of that one-month period, if the patient was free of diabetic symptoms, particularly ketoacidosis, the patient was randomly assigned to treatment and enrolled in the study.

Patients were asked to return every three months for follow-up examinations. At each quarterly follow-up examination, a general clinical examination and one of the detailed examinations for eyes, heart, kidney function, and peripheral vascular system were performed. The eye examination was performed in conjunction with the first quarterly examination of each year of follow-up. The heart examination was coupled with the second quarterly examination, the kidney function tests with the third, and the examination of the peripheral vascular system with the fourth. At each quarterly visit the patient was questioned concerning level of adherence to diet and the use of the assigned medication. The patient was also queried to determine all drugs prescribed and taken since the last completed quarterly follow-up examination and the reason each drug was prescribed. Each drug prescribed and taken was recorded at each quarterly examination and was coded according to a classification system5 modeled after the one outlined in A.M.A. Drug Evaluations.6 The reason each drug was prescribed was coded according to the system detailed in a previous report.5 Patients were queried to determine whether they had been hospitalized since the last completed follow-up examination; the duration in days and the reason for the hospitalization were recorded for each occurrence. Illnesses not requiring hospitalization were also recorded.

Analytic Procedures
The identification of a specified nonfatal event or condition that occurred during the course of the study was based on observations made at scheduled follow-up examinations. The only patients considered at risk of developing a specific condition or having a nonfatal event during the course of the study were those patients who met the following conditions:
(1) The patient was examined for or questioned about the condition (event) during the baseline examination and did not have the condition at that time and/or did not report a history of the event,
and
(2) The patient completed one or more follow-up examinations during which he/she was examined or questioned about the condition or event.

For example, 192 of the 205 patients in the placebo-treated group reported no prior history of angina pectoris at the baseline heart examination, but three of these patients did not complete any of the follow-up heart examinations. Thus, only 189 patients in the placebo treatment group were considered at risk of having angina pectoris for the first time during the course of the study and could have reported the occurrence of this event.

In all the analyses presented in this report only the first occurrence of the specified event was considered, so a patient was counted only once for each event. Review of the findings for the first occurrence of an event provided a means of screening for differences among the treatment groups. Whenever treatment differences were noted, additional analyses were performed to determine the persistence and the clinical importance of these findings.

The drug-placebo comparisons (TOLB versus PLBO, ISTD versus PLBO, and IVAR versus PLBO) of the number of patients who had a specified event or were identified with a specific condition for the first time during the course of follow-up were made with the Fisher exact test.7 In addition to the drug-placebo comparisons, the results for tolbutamide-treated patients have been compared with the results for insulin-treated patients since the primary question is which therapy should be regarded as the treatment of choice for adult-onset diabetes.

The P value obtained from the Fisher exact test represents the probability of obtaining the observed distribution or a more extreme distribution of patients with the event in each group under the null hypothesis of no difference in the occurrence of the event in the two groups being compared. However, these P values should be considered nominal P values; they provide only a measure of the relative magnitude of treatment differences, since no attempt has been made to adjust for the fact that several response variables have been considered for multiple treatment comparisons at frequent intervals during the course of the study. In our judgment, only P values less than 0.01 or perhaps 0.005 should be considered "statistically significant" in these circumstances.

In this report the results after the initiation of the assigned treatment for certain response variables are presented in terms of the per cent change from baseline for groups of patients (or cohorts) who have been followed for the same specified length of time. To be included in these analyses, a patient must have had an observation for the variable of interest at entry and at the last follow-up examination that defined the length of follow-up for the specified cohort. In addition, he/she must not have had two consecutive missing observations for the variable of interest. A missing value preceded and followed by an observed value was estimated by the mean of these two observed values.

Consideration of a specified cohort of patients has the advantage that any observed differences from one examination to another are assessed in the same group of patients. It is, of course, important that the cohorts considered should not be such a small proportion of the total number of patients in each treatment group that the results for the cohorts would not be representative of the results for the total groups.

RESULTS FROM THE HEART EXAMINATION
Specified Cardiovascular Events Identified at Follow-up Heart Examinations
A summary of the occurrence of events indicating heart disease or heart-related problems identified at follow-up heart examinations is given in part A of table 2. The percentage of patients identified during the course of follow-up with a specified nonfatal cardiac event or condition was higher in the tolbutamide-treated group than for patients in the placebo-treated group for each of the events listed in table 2 except for the occurrence of significant ECG abnormalities. The smallest P values from the Fisher exact tests of the drug-placebo comparisons were noted for the insulin standard versus placebo comparison for history of hospitalization for heart disease and the tolbutamide versus placebo comparison of use of digitalis. The tolbutamide versus insulin standard comparisons for history of hospitalization for heart disease and occurrence of angina pectoris yielded P values of 0.05. The insulin standard-treated group had fewer hospitalizations and fewer patients with angina pectoris than the placebo-treated group and the tolbutamide-treated group. More patients in the tolbutamide-treated group than in the placebo-treated group reported use of digitalis.

The percentages of patients identified with each of the specific ECG abnormalities (classified according to the Minnesota Code8,9 by graders who had no knowledge of treatment group or clinical findings for a patient) included in the category "significant ECG abnormalities" are given in table 3. The abnormalities most frequently identified in each treatment group were changes in Q-waves (Minnesota Codes 1-1-1 through 1-2-7, see Appendix A). Very few patients developed more than one ECG abnormality during the course of follow-up. This is evident from the fact that the sum of the percentages of the individual categories of abnormalities is only slightly greater than the percentage of patients identified with one or more of all the abnormalities listed. In the placebo-treated group, the sum of the percentages of patients with a specified abnormality was 13.1 per cent and the percentage of patients with one or more abnormalities was 12.6 per cent, indicating only one placebo-treated patient had more than one of these abnormalities. Similar results were noted for the patients in the other treatment groups. There were no patients in the tolbutamide-treated group with more than one of these abnormalities; one patient in the insulin standard treatment group and three patients in the insulin variable treatment group had more than one abnormality.

Myocardial Infarctions
The percentage of patients who reported having been hospitalized because of a myocardial infarction (MI) is given for each treatment group on the top line of part A of table 4. More patients in the placebo-treated group reported being hospitalized for a nonfatal myocardial infarction than did the patients in any of the other treatment groups.

The findings on the electrocardiograms obtained at follow-up heart examinations were compared with the ECG findings obtained at baseline for each of the patients considered in this report. Changes from a less severe finding for Q/QS and T patterns on the baseline ECG to a more severe finding for these abnormalities on a follow-up ECG were considered suggestive evidence that a nonfatal myocardial infarction had occurred some time after entry into the study. These changes are referred to as "significant worsening" in Q/QS or Q/QS and T patterns and are defined in Appendix A. The percentage of patients with Q/QS changes was highest in the insulin variable treatment group (6.3) and lowest in the tolbutamide treatment group (4.1). The percentages of patients with significant worsening of Q/QS and T patterns were almost the same in all four treatment groups, ranging from 2.0 for the insulin standard group to 4.1 for the tolbutamide group (table 4).

The persistence of these changes was determined by considering the number of patients who had these ECG changes noted on two consecutive ECGs obtained during follow-up. Forty-one patients were identified with Q/QS changes, and 12 of these had a second ECG with these same Q/QS changes. Among the 24 patients with Q/QS-plus-T-pattern changes at one examination, four had significant worsening observed on the ECG obtained at the next annual heart examination (table 4).

Reports of hospitalization for myocardial infarctions and significant worsening in the findings of the follow-up electrocardiograms were considered indicators that a patient had sustained a nonfatal myocardial infarction. The percentage of patients with one or more of these findings was slightly higher in the placebo-treated group than in the other three treatment groups.

The drug-placebo comparisons as well as the TOLB-ISTD and TOLB-IVAR comparisons for each of the events listed in table 4 were made with the Fisher exact test. The P values for these tests are given in part C of table 4. The smallest P value (0.04) was observed for the IVAR-PLBO comparison of hospitalizations for nonfatal MI.

The number of patients who had a nonfatal myocardial infarction (hospitalized for myocardial infarction or significant worsening of Q/QS or Q/QS and T patterns) during the course of follow-up is given for each treatment group on the second line of table 5. Fifty-one of these 67 patients were alive as of October 31, 1969. Five of these patients were reported to have died of myocardial infarction or sudden death as of October 31, 1969, and the remainder (11 patients) had died of other causes. A "sudden death" was defined as death occurring within three hours of the onset of symptoms in an otherwise clinically stable patient and in a manner consistent with a cardiovascular catastrophe. A death was classified as a fatal myocardial infarction if this diagnosis was made from ECG changes and changes in serum enzymes observed during the terminal course of illness or if the events leading to death were clinically compatible with this diagnosis and autopsy findings provided evidence that a myocardial infarction was the principal cause of death. The number of patients alive and the number dead due to myocardial infarction or sudden death among the patients identified with a nonfatal myocardial infarction are given for each treatment group on the third and fourth lines of table 5.

Patients who were hospitalized for a myocardial infarction and died or who died of a sudden death but who had not had a nonfatal myocardial infarction (as defined above) prior to the fatal event were classified in the category "fatal MI only." The number of these patients is given for each treatment group on the fifth line of table 5. The results for fatal and nonfatal myocardial infarctions have been pooled since the event is the same; only the outcome is different. The total number of patients who had either a fatal myocardial infarction, nonfatal myocardial infarction, or both is given on the sixth line of table 5. The percentage of patients identified with fatal or nonfatal myocardial infarction was approximately the same in all treatment groups (10.7 for PLBO, 13.7 for TOLB, 11.0 for ISTD, and 12.3 for IVAR).

The per cent mortality or case fatality for all patients who sustained a myocardial infarction may be determined on the basis of the information presented in table 5. For example, the mortality in the placebo treatment group was 18.2 per cent (2+2/22)X 100. The mortality in the other groups was 50.0 per cent for TOLB, 34.8 per cent for ISTD, and 40.0 per cent for IVAR. Although the occurrence of fatal and nonfatal myocardial infarctions was not different among the treatment groups, these results suggest that myocardial infarctions were more likely to be fatal for patients in the tolbutamide-treated group.

Use of Medications for Heart Disease or Heart-related Problems
The percentages of patients who reported taking certain medications for heart disease at one or more follow-up examinations during the course of the study are given for each treatment group in part A of table 6. Information on use of digitalis and history of angina pectoris was obtained for each patient during the baseline heart examination, and patients identified with a history of digitalis use or a history of angina pectoris were not considered at risk of use of digitalis or of antianginal drugs, respectively, in the analyses presented here. No information on use of other drugs was collected during the baseline examination; therefore, all patients who completed at least one quarterly follow-up examination were considered at risk of using one of the drugs considered in the analyses of other drugs for heart disease or heart-related problems.

The largest differences among the treatment groups were observed for the use of digitalis or drugs for congestive heart failure; fewer patients in the placebo-treated group than in any of the drug-treated groups reported taking digitalis or drugs for congestive heart failure. The Fisher exact test of the number of patients who reported taking digitalis and/or a drug for congestive heart failure at one or more follow-up examinations for the tolbutamide-placebo comparison and the insulin standard-placebo comparison yielded P values of 0.13 and 0.08, respectively.

Blood Pressure Levels During the Course of Follow-up
The per cent change from baseline in mean systolic blood pressure levels at each follow-up heart examination is plotted in figure 1 for the cohort of patients who were followed through the 18th follow-up examination. The mean systolic blood pressure level at entry in the cohorts of patients studied was 145 mm. Hg for PLBO, 143 mm. Hg for TOLB, 145 mm. Hg for ISTD, and 141 mm. Hg for IVAR. A slight decrease in systolic blood pressure was noted during the early period of follow-up. This initial decrease was followed by a rather constant but insignificant increase in all treatment groups.

The per cent change in mean diastolic blood pressure levels for the cohort of patients followed through the 18th follow-up examination is plotted for each treatment group in figure 2. The mean diastolic blood pressure level at baseline for each of these cohorts was 86 mm. Hg for PLBO, 84 mm. Hg for TOLB, 86 mm. Hg for ISTD, and 83 mm. Hg for IVAR. Although there were small increases in diastolic blood pressure levels after the initial decrease in all treatment groups, the mean levels remained below the baseline levels throughout the period of observation.

Occurrence of Hypertension and Treatment for It
The assessment of the development of hypertension during the course of the study was based on the occurrence of elevated blood pressure levels and the reporting of treatment for hypertension. These results are given in table 7. For this analysis, hypertension was defined according to the World Health Organization criteria:10 systolic blood pressure ≥160 mm. Hg and/or diastolic blood pressure ≥95 mm. Hg. The percentage of patients who had elevated blood pressure levels indicative of hypertension at one or more follow-up heart examinations and at two or more follow-up heart examinations is given for each treatment group in the first lines of parts A and B, respectively, of table 7.

The percentages of patients who were treated for hypertension at least once or at least twice during the course of follow-up among those patients who were not classified as hypertensive at baseline are given for each treatment group in the second lines of parts A and B of table 7. A patient was identified as receiving treatment for hypertension if at one or more quarterly follow-up examinations the patient reported he/she had been given a prescription for a specific antihypertensive drug or drugs or if the patient reported taking a medication for the treatment of hypertension even if the drug was not identified.

A smaller percentage of patients in the placebo-treated group than in the insulin-treated groups had elevated blood pressure levels or reported receiving treatment for hypertension whether the analysis was based on the occurrence at one or more examinations or at two or more examinations. The P values from the Fisher exact tests are given in part C of table 7. P values <0.10 were obtained for the PLBO-ISTD and PLBO-IVAR comparisons of treatment for hypertension at one or more examinations.

Review of the blood pressure readings for patients in the placebo and tolbutamide group who did not have elevated blood pressure levels at baseline or other indications of cardiovascular disease and who had at least three follow-up heart examinations also indicated no tolbutamide-placebo differences. Elevation of both systolic and diastolic blood pressure was observed in 12 patients in the placebo-treated group and 19 patients in the tolbutamide-treated group. Only 13 patients in each of these two groups ever had a systolic blood pressure reading greater than 180 mm. Hg; 14 patients in the placebo group and 20 in the tolbutamide group had one or more diastolic blood pressure readings greater than 100 mm. Hg.

Heart Rate
The mean heart rate (read from the ECG) at baseline for the cohort of patients completing the 18th follow-up examination was 70 beats per minute for PLBO, 70 beats per minute for TOLB, 71 beats per minute for ISTD, and 72 beats per minute for IVAR. All changes in heart rate in this cohort during the course of follow-up were within 2 per cent of the baseline levels (figure 3).

Cardiothoracic Ratio
The mean cardiothoracic ratio at baseline for patients in the cohort followed through the 20th follow-up examination was approximately equal in all treatment groups (0.48 for PLBO, 0.47 for TOLB, 0.48 for ISTD, and 0.48 for IVAR). The changes from baseline in all treatment groups were 2 per cent to 3 per cent above baseline levels during the final periods of observation with the exception of patients in the placebo-treated group who showed even less change from baseline (figure 4).

RESULTS FROM OTHER EXAMINATIONS
Findings Obtained During Follow-up Eye Examinations
The percentage of patients identified with an eye condition during the course of follow-up is given for each treatment group in part A of table 8. Each of the drug-placebo comparisons for the conditions listed in table 8 was obtained with the Fisher exact test. The smallest P value (0.13) was obtained for the insulin standard versus placebo comparison of the number of patients identified with exudates in the fundus photographs; more patients in the insulin standard-treated group had evidence of exudates than did patients in the placebo-treated group. Exudates were considered separately since it was not always possible for the reader to differentiate true exudates from artifacts appearing on the film or from drusen bodies found in normal retinae. None of the other comparisons yielded P values less than 0.20 (part C of table 8).

The specific abnormalities, excluding exudates, that were identified in the right central fundus photographs are given in part A of table 9. Other than exudates, the findings that were observed most frequently in the fundus photographs were retinal hemorrhages and microaneurysms. Proliferative changes and neovascularization based on the grading of the central right photograph were identified in only two patients, one patient in the placebo treatment group and one in the insulin standard treatment group.

Fundus photographs were not obtained for all patients at the baseline examination because of technical problems with the Zeiss camera. Therefore, an analysis was also performed in which either the baseline photographs or those taken at quarter 1 (three months after entry) were used to define the baseline status for each patient. In this analysis the identification of the new findings during the course of follow-up was based on all follow-up eye examinations excluding the results from the eye examination completed at quarter 1. The results of this analysis are summarized in part B of table 9, and they are similar to the results obtained when only baseline findings were used to classify the patient's status at baseline.

Only small changes from the baseline levels were noted in all treatment groups during this period of observation (figure 5).

The smallest P value (0.06) was obtained for the TOLB versus ISTD comparison for urine protein levels ≥1 gm. per L. None of the other comparisons yielded P values less than 0.10.

Findings Obtained During Follow-up Peripheral Vascular Examinations
A summary of conditions identified at follow-up peripheral vascular examinations is given in part A of table 11. Two patients in the placebo-treated group and one patient in the insulin variable-treated group had an amputation of a lower limb during the course of follow-up. The reported reason for the amputation was gangrene in every instance. The percentage of patients identified with arterial calcification was highest in the insulin variable-treated group and lowest in the placebo-treated group. More patients were identified with intermittent claudication in the tolbutamide-treated group than in any of the other treatment groups. The TOLB-PLBO comparison for this event yielded the smallest P value (0.08).

Body Weight
The changes in body weight for patients in each of the treatment groups considered in this report are presented in figure 6. For this analysis, changes in body weight have been evaluated in terms of relative body weight, which was defined as the ratio of a patient's observed body weight to his/her desirable body weight. Desirable body weight was derived from tables published by the Medical Society of the County of New York.11 Patients in all treatment groups at the time of entry into the study were on the average 34 per cent overweight. After the initiation of treatment, which included a prescription for diet with caloric content designed to achieve or to maintain the patient's body weight within 15 per cent of the desirable amount, there was a small initial loss of weight for patients in all treatment groups. The initial weight loss was maintained only for patients in the placebo-treated group.

Serum Cholesterol Levels
The per cent change in serum cholesterol levels from baseline to each follow-up examination is presented in figure 7 for the cohort of patients in each treatment group followed through the 20th follow-up examination. The mean serum cholesterol levels at baseline for patients followed through the 20th follow-up examination was 229 mg. per 100 ml. for PLBO, 236 mg. per 100 ml. for TOLB, 240 mg. per 100 ml. for ISTD, and 241 mg. per 100 ml. for IVAR. A decrease of 3.3 per cent during the first year of follow-up was noted for patients in the insulin variable-treated group whereas there was an increase of 3.0 per cent for patients in the placebo-treated group. After five years of follow-up (i.e., at the 20th follow-up examination) there was an increase of 7.7 per cent in the placebo-treated group; smaller increases were noted for patients in the tolbutamide- and insulin standard-treated groups, and the mean value for the insulin variable-treated patients had just returned to the baseline level.

Use of Other Medications
The percentages of patients who reported taking specific types of drugs during the course of follow-up are given in table 12. The findings for the use of drugs for heart disease or heart-related problems were presented in table 6.

More patients in the placebo-treated group than in the insulin standard-treated group reported taking erythromycin or derivatives and aspirin-containing analgesics (P values for these comparisons were <0.05, indicated by daggers in table 12). More patients in the insulin standard-treated group than in the placebo-treated group reported taking adrenal corticosteroids and corticotropin (P value <0.05). Four of the tests of TOLB versus PLBO comparisons yielded P values <0.10 but ≥0.05 (indicated by an asterisk in table 12). These P values were obtained from the comparisons of agents used in gout, antihistamines, cold remedies, and peripheral vasodilators, and, except for the use of antihistamines, more patients in the tolbutamide treatment group than in the placebo treatment group reported taking the indicated medication.

An analysis of the reported reasons for taking specified drugs is given in table 13. The largest differences noted for the TOLB-versus-PLBO comparisons were in the reported use of drugs taken for gastrointestinal infections and of those taken for unspecified thyroid maladies. A larger percentage of patients in the tolbutamide group reported taking medication for unspecified thyroid maladies, but a smaller percentage in this group reported taking medication for gastrointestinal infections. The Fisher exact tests of these comparisons yielded P values <0.10 but ≥0.05. The Fisher exact test for the ISTD versus PLBO comparison of the reported use of drugs for trauma and environmental exposure (7.4 per cent in PLBO and 2.9 per cent in ISTD) yielded a P value <0.10 but ≥0.05. Patients in the insulin variable-treated group reported taking fewer medications for abscesses and inflammations and for female genital infections than patients in the placebo-treated group (P value <0.10 but ≥0.05 from the Fisher exact tests of both of these comparisons).

Cancer
Information on the occurrence of cancer was obtained by reviewing the information on hospitalizations recorded on the quarterly follow-up examination forms. The percentage of patients who reported hospitalization for cancer is given on the second line of part A of table 14. The percentage of patients who reported hospitalization for cancer and/or who had died and the coded cause of death was cancer is given on the fifth line of part A of table 14. Fewer patients in the tolbutamide treatment group than in the placebo treatment group had cancer; the Fisher exact test of the tolbutamide-placebo comparison of the number of patients with cancer yielded a P value of 0.03. The P value for the TOLB versus IVAR comparison of any report of cancer was 0.06; more patients in the insulin variable-treated group than in the tolbutamide-treated group reported having cancer.

BLOOD GLUCOSE LEVELS
Analysis of the mean changes in fasting blood glucose levels for the cohort of patients followed through the 19th follow-up examination indicated-as might be expected-that variable doses of insulin were more effective in maintaining reduced blood glucose levels over the period covered by this analysis than any of the other hypoglycemic treatments studied. The fasting blood glucose values were obtained as part of the short GTT (details of the methods for this test are given in the paper on design, methods, and baseline results1). As may be seen from figure 8, there was a large drop in glucose levels in all treatment groups after the initiation of treatment. At the end of the first follow-up period, patients in the placebo-treated group showed a 12.9 per cent reduction in fasting glucose levels from the baseline mean in contrast to a 24.9 per cent reduction in the tolbutamide-treated group, a 20.7 per cent reduction in the insulin standard-treated group, and a 22.2 per cent reduction in the insulin variable-treated group. Patients in these cohorts showed a gradual return toward the baseline levels in all treatment groups, except for those in the insulin variable-treated group. The mean fasting blood glucose level recorded at the 19th follow-up examination for patients in the insulin variable treatment group was 19.5 per cent lower than at baseline. The corresponding values for the other treatment groups were 13.7 per cent above baseline for PLBO, 2.3 per cent above baseline for TOLB, and 4.3 per cent below baseline for ISTD.

The lower fasting blood glucose levels in the insulin variable-treated group were maintained during the course of follow-up by increasing the number of units of insulin prescribed. The mean number of units of insulin prescribed for patients in the insulin variable treatment group was 12 at the first quarterly follow-up examination and 35 at the 19th quarterly follow-up examination.

An analysis of blood glucose control was based on definitions of glucose control derived from those used by the members of the Joslin Clinic.12 These definitions are outlined in table 15. The percentage of patients who had GOOD control was almost identical for the tolbutamide- and insulin standard-treated groups, 37.4 for TOLB and 37.6 for ISTD. The highest levels of control were achieved in the insulin variable-treated group; 48.0 per cent were classified as having GOOD control and only 11.6 per cent were classified as having POOR control. The percentage of patients with POOR control in the other treatment groups was 41.2 for PLBO, 25.6 for TOLB, and 28.3 for ISTD.

ADHERENCE TO TREATMENT
The percentage of patients in each treatment group who took ALL of the assigned study medication for at least 75 per cent of all follow-up periods through October 31, 1969, is given in part A of table 16. For this calculation a patient was regarded as having taken ALL of the study medication in a given follow-up period if he/she was given a prescription for the assigned study medication in the dosage specified by protocol and if he/she took that medication for at least 75 days of that three-month period and did not take any hypoglycemic agent other than the assigned study medication during that period. This assessment of level of adherence was based on the patient's prescription record and the physician's judgment of the patient's adherence to that prescription. Nearly three-fourths (74 per cent) of the patients in the tolbutamide treatment group took ALL of their study medication for at least 75 per cent of the follow-up periods prior to the quarterly follow-up examination at which the prescription for tolbutamide was discontinued. The corresponding percentage for the placebo treatment group was 68.3 per cent. The lowest levels of adherence were noted in the insulin-treated groups (58.6 per cent for ISTD and 47.5 per cent for IVAR).

The per cent of patients who took ALL or SOME of the prescribed study medication for at least 75 per cent of all follow-up periods prior to the quarterly examination at which the protocol was changed is given in part B of table 16. For this analysis a patient was regarded as having taken SOME of the assigned medication if the patient was given a prescription for the assigned medication but for a dosage different from that specified by protocol (below or above that specified by protocol) and took this dosage for at least 75 days of the 90 days in that follow-up period or had a prescription for the exact dose specified by protocol and took this dose for at least 15 days but less than 75 days and if the patient had not taken any other hypoglycemic medication during this period. If the assessment of level of adherence is based on the per cent of follow-up periods in which patients took ALL or SOME of the prescribed medication, the results for the two insulin treatment groups are very similar to the results for the placebo and tolbutamide treatment groups.

The per cent of patients who took little or none of the assigned study medication during the entire time they were under study may be obtained from the last line of part B of table 16-that is, the percentage of patients who took ALL or SOME of the assigned study medication for less than 25 per cent of all follow-up periods. The per cent of patients who were classified in this way was 9.3 for PLBO, 8.3 for TOLB, 11.0 for ISTD, and 10.3 for IVAR.

LITERATURE REVIEW
A summary of animal studies, autopsy studies, and three prospective studies determining the long-term efficacy of tolbutamide was included in a previous UGDP report.2 Since that report was published, Lasseter and co-workers13 have shown an inotropic effect in human heart muscle on cardiac contractions and an increase in cardiac irritability due to tolbutamide and other sulfonylurea drugs. Wales, Grant, and Wolff14 reported observing increased blood pressure levels in dogs following tolbutamide administration.

A report from the Kaiser-Permanente Drug Monitoring System indicated that there were more cases of congestive heart failure among tolbutamide users than among nonusers.15 This difference was not statistically significant.

In a six-year prospective study of 186 newly diagnosed adult-onset diabetics by Boyle and co-workers,16 the 71 patients treated predominantly with oral antidiabetic agents had a greater frequency of myocardial infarctions than the 115 patients treated predominantly with diet. These findings should be interpreted with some caution since patients were not randomly allocated to treatment but rather treatment was determined by the patient's blood sugar response.

The mortality experience of 184 patients with acute myocardial infarcts, confirmed by ECG and serum enzyme changes, and a known history of diabetes was reported recently by Soler et al.17 At the time of admission to a coronary care unit, 27 of these 184 patients were being treated with dietary restrictions only and had never had any antidiabetic treatment other than diet. Ninety of the 184 patients were taking oral hypoglycemic agents (chlorpropamide, tolbutamide, glibenclamide, metformin, or a combination of a sulfonylurea and metformin), and 67 patients were taking insulin at the time of admission to the hospital. Patients on diet alone had an observed mortality of 15 per cent in the hospital, but in the groups receiving prescriptions for oral agents or insulin the observed mortality in the hospital was 40 per cent and 37 per cent, respectively. The mortality was slightly higher in the group treated with oral drugs even though the patients in the insulin group had a higher incidence of arterial hypertension, angina, and heart failure as well as retinopathy on admission with an MI and had been receiving antidiabetic treatment for a longer period of time. Mean duration of treatment with insulin was 12.9 years. The mean duration of treatment with oral drugs was 5.7 years, preceded by an average of 1.7 years of treatment with diet alone. These investigators also reported that 12 per cent of the patients receiving oral hypoglycemic drugs had primary ventricular fibrillation, as against 3 per cent of patients receiving insulin and 7 per cent of patients on diet alone. Although the number of patients in each of the groups was small and the lack of random assignment to treatment poses problems in the interpretation of these findings, these findings are consistent with observations made by Lasseter and Palmer that showed an increase in cardiac irritability if a patient is taking one of the sulfonylureas and with the observation in the UGDP that there was an increased likelihood of a fatal outcome if the patient has a myocardial infarction and is taking tolbutamide than if the patient suffers this event and is taking insulin or no hypoglycemic agent.

Of the three prospective studies18-20 reviewed in the earlier UGDP report,2 only the study conducted by Keen and co-workers18,21 has reported results on nonfatal vascular events. The results from this study will be compared with the findings from the UGDP in the next section.

The most recent report of the study of tolbutamide and phenformin conducted by Feldman and co-workers19 noted that after 5.5 years of observation two deaths had occurred among 350 patients (174 assigned to tolbutamide treatment, 91 to phenformin treatment, and 85 to placebo treatment). No information on nonfatal events was presented.

Results from the Paasikivi study20 of postcoronary patients indicated a lower mortality for patients treated with tolbutamide than for the placebo-treated group only during the first six months of follow-up. In the succeeding four years of follow-up the observed mortality in the tolbutamide-treated group was greater than that in the placebo-treated group. One reviewer concluded that "the report by J. Paasikivi in postcoronary patients is contradicting in its data and can more properly be interpreted as supporting the findings of the UGDP."22

COMPARISON OF RESULTS FROM BEDFORD STUDY AND RESULTS FROM UGDP
In all that has been written to date concerning the use of sulfonylurea drugs there is no evidence of beneficial effects associated with long-term use of tolbutamide. Although the early report by Keen and co-workers18 on the Bedford study has been cited as providing evidence of beneficial results from tolbutamide, this was not borne out by later analyses by these investigators.21 Patients in this study were randomly assigned to placebo or to tolbutamide therapy. The 123 patients assigned to tolbutamide therapy received a fixed dosage of 1.0 gm. of tolbutamide per day (0.5 gm. twice daily). The remaining 125 patients were treated with "placebo" tablets (two per day) each containing 3 mg. of tolbutamide. Each of these subgroups was further subdivided so that half were advised to reduce carbohydrate intake to 120 gm. daily and the other half only to reduce their intake of sugar. Every six months, patients returned for examination and a variety of standard measurements were made. Analysis of cardiovascular events observed during the first five years of follow-up indicated that the group treated with tolbutamide and advised to restrict carbohydrate intake had fewer cardiovascular events than did the patients in any of the other three groups. The number of events in the individual groups was small, but the percentage of patients with probable events in the two tolbutamide-treated groups (22 per cent) was lower (P value less than 0.05 without adjustment for multiple testing) than observed in the two placebo-treated groups (33 per cent).

At the end of seven years of follow-up the proportion of patients with one or more cardiovascular events was lower in the tolbutamide treatment group than in the placebo treatment group, but Keen and co-workers did not consider this difference "significantly" lower (P value<0.10 but ≥0.05 without adjustment).21 For both these analyses, the investigators included the following events: probable myocardial infarction, probable angina pectoris, probable intermittent claudication, ECG worsening based on the Minnesota Code, stroke, and cardiovascular death.

In all previous UGDP reports the assessment of treatment effects has been based primarily on differences in the occurrence of single events since the interpretation of differences in treatment effects based on pooling events ranging in severity from intermittent claudication to death is not perspicuous. However, to provide the reader with results for comparison with the Bedford study, the findings for the same cardiovascular events (although there may be some minor differences in definitions) are presented for UGDP patients in part II of table 17; the reported results from the Bedford study are given in part I of the same table. The average length of follow-up in the UGDP, as of October 31, 1969, was approximately five years. Some patients had completed eight years and some less than four years, but 654 of the 823 patients studied had completed five years. The average length of follow-up in the Bedford study was seven years for the results presented in part I of table 17.

In the Bedford study the percentage of patients with each of the nonfatal cardiovascular events (based on all patients allocated to treatment, since no information is presented by Keen on the presence of these events at entry into the study) was higher in the placebo-treated group than in the tolbutamide-treated group except for myocardial infarctions and intermittent claudication. The percentage of patients with any fatal or nonfatal cardiovascular event was also higher for placebo-treated patients than for tolbutamide-treated patients, as noted previously. The findings for patients in the UGDP showed quite different trends. The percentage of patients with each of the nonfatal events (based on patients who did not have the event at baseline) was higher for patients in the tolbutamide-treated group than for patients in the placebo-treated group except for the occurrence of nonfatal myocardial infarctions and ECG worsening. The percentage of patients with any fatal or nonfatal cardiovascular event in the UGDP was 26.8 per cent for PLBO and 35.3 per cent for TOLB, in contrast to the Bedford study findings (37.4 for PLBO and 28.0 for TOLB).

It is unlikely that the results of two prospective studies would be identical since there are differences in design, in the patients studied, in definitions of endpoints, and in the hypoglycemic effects achieved by the specified treatments. Patients enrolled in the Bedford study were classified as "borderline" diabetics and would generally be considered to have less glucose intolerance or a milder disease than the patients studied in the UGDP. The patients in the Bedford study were about 10 years younger at the time of entry into the study than the UGDP patients, yet a larger proportion of patients in the placebo treatment group than in the tolbutamide treatment group in the Bedford study were over age 70 (29.6 per cent in PLBO, 17.9 per cent in TOLB, P value <0.05). Fixed doses of tolbutamide were used in both studies, but in the UGDP 1.5 gm. per day (the level most commonly used in clinical practice1) and in the Keen study 1.0 gm. per day was prescribed. Analysis of the results for capillary blood sugar levels two hours after a 50-gm. challenge indicated no differences among treatment groups in the Bedford study during the course of follow-up; this indicated no effect at all on two-hour postchallenge blood sugar of 1.0 gm. of tolbutamide, while a marked hypoglycemic effect was obtained with the dose used in the UGDP. Whether these differences or random variation account for the conflicting results from these two studies is unclear.

Perhaps it should be noted that the placebo group in the Bedford study took a small amount of tolbutamide. Although there is little likelihood that this had an effect on blood glucose, it is not clear what effect the presence of a potentially sensitizing or allergenic agent might have. Bloodworth et al.23 found microgranulomata in heart muscle of patients treated with sulfonylurea agents. These findings have not been confirmed by others;24,25 thus, it seems unlikely that the detrimental effects of tolbutamide are due to sensitization. However, if this was the case, then no differences would be expected in the groups studied by Keen, since both the treated group and the control group received some of the agent.

The Committee for the Assessment of Biometric Aspects of Controlled Clinical Trials of Hypoglycemic Agents26 concluded that the only mortality data extensive enough to determine the efficacy of these agents were those from the UGDP study and the Bedford study. This committee noted that many of the criticisms leveled against the UGDP would apply "sometimes even more strongly, to the Bedford study, but it was the UGDP trial that was challenged by the critics." After reviewing the published evidence available at the end of 1972 and reanalyzing some of the data from the UGDP and Bedford studies, the Biometrics Committee concluded "we consider that, in the light of the UGDP findings, it remains with the proponents of the oral [agents] to conduct scientifically adequate studies to justify the continued use of such agents."

DISCUSSION AND SUMMARY
In this report, a review of the development of nonfatal events has been presented for patients assigned to placebo, tolbutamide, insulin standard, or insulin variable based on observations reported at all quarterly examinations completed up to and including the quarterly examinations at which tolbutamide therapy was discontinued for tolbutamide patients and the examinations completed in the same interval for patients in the other treatment groups. The emphasis in the earlier UGDP reports on tolbutamide therapy was on the analysis of mortality data because results for nonfatal events are not so important if significant differences among the treatment groups have been observed for mortality. Review of all the findings for fatal and nonfatal events provides no evidence of benefit associated with the long-term use of tolbutamide.

The comparability of the treatment groups was established in earlier reports. More important, the observed tolbutamide-placebo differences in mortality and cardiovascular mortality changed very little when the baseline characteristics of the patients in these groups were taken into account in the analysis.2,26,27

Of course, it was not possible to adjust for risk factors not measured, such as smoking history. However, the effect of an extreme difference in the composition of the placebo and tolbutamide groups with respect to smoking history, such as 30 per cent in PLBO and 50 per cent in TOLB, which has only one chance in 50,000 of occurring if 40 per cent of all UGDP patients were smokers, could account for only 1.6 cardiovascular deaths or only 16 per cent of the observed excess cardiovascular mortality.27

Except for blood glucose levels, few differences in the occurrence of nonfatal events or conditions determined at scheduled follow-up examinations were observed among the groups treated with diet alone, tolbutamide and diet, or insulin and diet in the UGDP. The fasting blood glucose levels in the tolbutamide-treated group were lower than the levels in the diet alone group and about the same as the levels in the group treated with a fixed dose of insulin; patients treated with variable doses of insulin had consistently lower levels than the patients in the other three groups (figure 8 and table 15.). None of the treatments had much effect on body weight, although the patients in the group treated with diet alone lost a little more weight than the patients in the other groups (figure 6). In contrast to the findings for body weight, a small decrease in serum cholesterol levels was observed in the insulin variable treatment group and a small increase in the placebo treatment group after one year of follow-up (figure 7). The serum cholesterol levels in the insulin variable-treated group remained below the baseline levels for almost five years of follow-up. During this same period further increases were observed in the placebo-treated group. The possibility of long-term prevention of coronary heart disease as a result of the effect of insulin on cholesterol levels in the insulin variable-treated group cannot be dismissed, but the observed cardiovascular mortality in these two treatment groups during the period of follow-up covered by this analysis provided no significant evidence of a beneficial effect of variable doses of insulin.

Regardless of the definition used for hypertension, the trends were about the same in all treatment groups, indicating no effect of blood glucose changes on blood pressure levels. Fewer patients in the placebo-treated group than in any of the other groups reported taking digitalis or medication for congestive heart failure (table 6). An increased risk of congestive heart failure among diabetics compared with nondiabetics was observed in the Framingham study.28 Extensive analysis of the Framingham data indicated that the excessive risk was confined to subjects treated with insulin; no discernible risk was observed for subjects treated with tolbutamide or diet. In the UGDP the reported use of digitalis or medication for congestive heart failure by patients in the tolbutamide treatment group was almost the same as that for patients in the insulin standard treatment group but slightly more than that for patients in the insulin variable treatment group.

The occurrence of myocardial infarctions as well as the other vascular changes studied in this report was about the same in all treatment groups even though differences in blood glucose levels were observed. These results suggest that attempts to normalize blood glucose levels in the adult-onset diabetic will not alter the incidence of renal impairment, retinal changes, or the other common complications of diabetes. Of course, it must be recognized that few patients were found to have renal or ocular disorders over the period covered by this report, and longer periods of follow-up may yield different results with respect to these vascular changes.

Fewer patients in the tolbutamide treatment group than in the other three groups were hospitalized for cancer or died and cancer was considered the primary cause of death (table 14). The Fisher exact test of the TOLB versus PLBO and TOLB versus IVAR comparisons of the occurrence of cancer yielded P values < 0.10. These findings suggest the principle of competing risks. Thus, if patients treated with tolbutamide had more severe myocardial infarctions, it may be assumed that they did not have a chance to develop cancer.

The excess mortality observed in the tolbutamide-treated group over that of patients treated with diet or diet plus insulin appeared to be a result of increased mortality among tolbutamide-treated patients who had sustained a myocardial infarction (table 5). The mortality for all patients who had at least one myocardial infarction during the course of follow-up was 50 per cent for patients in the tolbutamide-treated group and 18 per cent for patients in the placebo-treated group. The mortality for such patients in the insulin-treated groups was 35 per cent for ISTD and 40 per cent for IVAR. These observations are consistent with the observations that sulfonylurea drugs may produce an enhanced inotropic effect on heart muscle and increased automaticity of the Purkinje fibers, since these factors may increase the extent of ischemic myocardial damage and lead to arrhythmias.13 The already mentioned report17 from Great Britain of a greater occurrence of primary ventricular fibrillation among patients receiving prescriptions for sulfonylurea and/or biguanide drugs than for patients receiving prescriptions for insulin on admission to a coronary care unit because of a myocardial infarction add further support to this explanation of the observed excess cardiovascular mortality in the tolbutamide-treated group.
